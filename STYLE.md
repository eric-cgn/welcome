# Welcome to the Internet: style guide

The rules for every word, page, and picture in the guide. Agree on changes here before the pages
change.

## What the guide is

A short guide to the Internet from OneInAMillion Software, the makers of Mill. It explains what the
Internet is, protocol by protocol, using the ones Mill speaks, and gives the reader real places to
visit. It is a welcome for Mill users and a starting point for anyone exploring the Internet from a
Compact Apple Macintosh.

The guide and the Welcome letter are written for people who use Mill, to teach them what they have
and help them make full use of it. We think other readers will benefit too, and we invite readers
on any computer to browse and enjoy the guide.

- **"You" is a Mill user on a Macintosh.** Instructions are written for Mill, and sentences such as
  "Your Macintosh puts the parcels back in order" are exactly true for that reader. Don't hedge each
  sentence for other readers.
- **The Welcome letter invites everyone else once,** plainly, and nowhere else needs to.
- **Mill is freeware.** Readers use or choose Mill; they never buy or purchase it.

## The reader

### Primary reader, in 1997

Write for this person first.

- **Their computer:** a Compact Macintosh bought new around 1990 to 1992 as a sensible, affordable
  first computer. Most likely a Macintosh Classic: 9-inch black-and-white screen, a few megabytes of
  memory, a small hard disk, System 6 or 7. Possibly a hand-me-down Plus or SE. This is the machine
  Mill is built for.
- **What they use it for:** letters, school reports, household accounts, perhaps a club newsletter.
- **What they're comfortable with:** the Finder, the mouse, menus, windows, folders, documents,
  saving, floppy disks. They learned from Apple's manuals and the tour that came with the computer.
- **What they've heard:** "the Internet," "the information superhighway," and Web addresses in
  advertisements. They have never used it, or have only watched someone else.
- **How they connect:** they're newly connected, by modem through a local Internet provider or
  through the network at school or work. They chose Mill because it runs on the computer they already
  own.
- **Temperament:** intelligent and careful, a little wary of breaking something, and unwilling to
  feel foolish. They'd rather read slowly than guess.
- **What they don't know yet:** what a link is, that underlined words can be clicked, or what a
  server, protocol, or address is. They don't know there are places on the Internet besides the Web.

Explaining the basics to this reader is respectful, not insulting, as long as it is done plainly
and without apology, the way Apple's manuals explained clicking and dragging.

### Secondary reader, today

Someone exploring the Internet from a Compact Macintosh who knows modern computers but has never
looked underneath them, at protocols, servers, and addresses. Writing for the primary reader serves
this reader too. Never write for them at the primary reader's expense.

Readers who knew the Internet well and are coming back also read the guide. They enjoy the period
details (see *Nostalgia*). Never talk down to them, and never write for them at the primary reader's
expense either.

## The rule: true then, true now

Every sentence must pass two tests:

1. **Then:** it could appear in a guide printed for Christmas 1997.
2. **Now:** every claim in it is true for the person reading it today.

Never break the fourth wall. When something about today doesn't fit the period, find the wording
that is true in both years. Never comment on the gap between them.

### Techniques

| Technique | Not this | This |
|---|---|---|
| State the trend, not the outcome | Most sites now require HTTPS. | Some sites already use encryption, and the trend is likely to continue. |
| Present today's fixes as services the reader can get | Use a modern proxy to reach today's sites. | Working with encryption requires a powerful computer, so many Internet providers also include a service called a proxy. |
| Name things by what they are, not their era | classic Macs, vintage hardware, mid-'90s browsers | Compact Apple Macintosh computers; a Macintosh with a 68000 processor |
| Say who keeps a thing going, not whether it's fading | Gopher is mostly gone. | Many Gopher servers are run by people who prefer its plain menus to the Web's pages. |
| Describe by kind, not by size | millions of sites; the largest archive | more sites than anyone could visit |
| Put shared networks at school or work | your home network | If you connect through the network at your school or office... |
| Name the specialist tool, then show what Mill can do | Mill can't send mail. | While specialized programs exist to manage electronic mail, Mill can... |
| Explain pages Mill can't show by describing the page | Today's sites won't render. | Some sites are designed for more powerful computers with color screens. Mill shows what it can. |
| Describe sign-ups and limits as a server's policy | Few free news servers are left. | Many news servers ask you to sign up for an account. |
| Describe privacy as a choice | Nobody runs finger anymore. | Some computers answer finger requests. Many don't, to protect their users' privacy. |

### Words that date the text

These words imply that time has passed, so they never appear in the guide's own text:

- Relative to the reader's time: *still, anymore, nowadays, today's, modern, these days, back then,
  once, used to, remain(s), survive(s), no longer*
- Labels for the reader's equipment: *retro, vintage, classic, old, legacy, obsolete, antique*
- Words from after 1997: *app, cloud, social media, stream(ing), smartphone, tablet, Wi-Fi, Google,
  blog, wiki, podcast, emoji*

A word in these lists may be used only when it describes the topic, not the reader's time ("the oldest
newsgroups"). Mark each such use for review (see *Marking judgment calls*).

### Dates

History may name years up to 1997 ("Gopher was written at the University of Minnesota in 1991").
Never name a later year, and never use relative time ("a few years ago", "recently").

## The company

OneInAMillion Software is the only fiction. Everything else is real: every fact, every link, every
description of how something works, and every piece of history.

Say as little about the company as possible. Every detail we commit to closes off something a reader
might imagine. The company makes Mill and wishes the reader well. Never give its founding date,
address, size, staff, history, or other people. "We" in the company voice is fine because it commits
to nothing.

No invented people, quotes, testimonials, awards, or press.

## The narrator

- The **Welcome** page is a signed letter in the first person from Eric McIntyre, Founder,
  OneInAMillion Software. It ends with `images/signature.gif`, then his typed name and title.
- Every other page is in the company voice: "we" for OneInAMillion, "you" for the reader.

The reference opening:

> On behalf of OneInAMillion Software, welcome to the Internet.

> and your computer could read it because both follow the same published rules. Those rules are what
> the Internet is made of, and this guide is about them.

## Voice

Model the voice on the best plain guides of the period, which explained patiently and generously and
never hyped. Think of a well-read friend at the next desk in a university computer lab.

- **Precise.** Every sentence is literally true, including its comparisons and contrasts. Never trade
  accuracy for rhythm. A contrast must not deny something true: "useful not because it is fast" is
  wrong when speed is part of the reason; write "useful not only because it is fast, but also
  because...". Absolutes (*any, every, never, all*) must hold exactly, or be narrowed until they do.
  Technical details inside a comparison must be right too: MacTCP puts packets in order, not Mill.
- **Fun to read.** Precision and plainness are no excuse for dullness.
- **Plain words, short sentences, active voice.** Address the reader as "you."
- **Warm but precise.** Delight is welcome; hype is not. No exclamation marks, no superlatives, no
  "amazing" or "awesome".
- **Concrete first.** Start with an address to type and what the reader will see, then explain it.
- **Define each term once**, in one sentence, where it first appears. The glossary has the rest.
- **Never "simply", "just", "easy", or "obviously".** What is easy for us may not be easy for the
  reader.
- **One metaphor per idea**, and say where it stops working. A wrong mental model costs the reader
  more than no metaphor at all.
- **The reader's computer is never old.** It is a Macintosh with these capabilities: "If your
  Macintosh has 1 MB of memory..."
- **The reader is new to the Internet, not to computers.** Build out from the Finder, files, and
  menus they already know.
- **No sneering and no preaching** about other sites or companies. Show what is out there and let
  it speak for itself.
- **No nostalgia, no irony, and no winking** at the reader about their computer's age.

### Pace: the way Apple's manuals teach

The reader may not know what the Internet is. Take it slowly, and treat the reader with respect: assume
intelligence, never knowledge. Apple's Macintosh manuals are the model.

- **One new idea at a time,** each built on one already explained. Never use a term before it has
  been explained. If an explanation needs another idea first, explain that idea first, or move the
  explanation to a later page.
- **Start from what the reader knows:** the Finder, documents, folders, menus, the mouse.
- **One name for each thing.** Once something has a name, always use that name. Never switch to a
  synonym: a protocol is never a "service", a server never a "site" or "host", an address never a
  "location" or "URL" (except to explain that word once in the glossary).
- **Remind the reader where a term came from** when it returns from an earlier page: "As you read in
  Rules of the Road, a protocol is..."
- **Explain why, not only what.**
- **Steps the reader performs are numbered,** one action each, followed by what the reader will see.
  The first page that asks the reader to type an address gives every step; later pages can say
  "Type".
- **The ordinary case comes first.** Exceptions and variations (other ports, addresses without a
  server) come after the reader understands the ordinary case, and are labeled as such.
- **When a page is getting long, split it** rather than hurry. Two calm pages beat one rushed one.

### The central idea

The Internet is not a site. It is a set of published rules, called protocols, that any computer can
learn. This is why a Compact Macintosh can talk to any computer on the Internet, and why anyone can
run a server as well as visit one. Every page should leave the reader understanding this a little
better.

### Analogies: use them, don't torture them

An analogy is a way in, not a place to stay. Use it while it helps, and drop it once the reader has
the idea. After a concept has been explained, call it by its real name: request, server, packet,
protocol.

- **The guide's one extended analogy is the information superhighway,** on the Rules of the Road
  page. We take it somewhere new: a highway is useful not only because it is fast, but also because
  everyone follows the same rules of the road, which is what protocols are. The computers do the
  driving, as in the cartoon, and they run a relay: the reader's Macintosh carries the note (the
  request) over the first leg, and each computer along the way (a router) carries it over the next,
  toward the store (the server). The reply comes back the same way in parcels (packets) kept small
  enough for every car's trunk. The page ends by naming the real terms, and the analogy is not
  carried further.
- **Don't extend it to everything.** Proxies, encryption, and name lookup (DNS) are explained plainly
  or with their own short comparison on their own pages, never as more highway or relay detail.
  When an extension would need explaining itself, leave it out.
- **Each protocol page uses the comparison that fits best,** such as a card catalog for Gopher or a
  bulletin board for newsgroups, and only one.
- **The highway stays out of the Welcome letter,** apart from at most one line.
- **Section names may mention the highway sparingly** ("Rules of the Road"). Mark each one EDGE.

### Mill, honestly

Say plainly what Mill doesn't do, and don't sell short what it does. Name the specialist programs,
then show what Mill can do. Never invent a capability. Check each claim against Mill's README and
change log.

### Humor

An occasional clever remark or inside joke is welcome if it fits; don't resist one that does. Never
make one the point of a passage. Mark anything near the line for review.

### Nostalgia

Take the chance for nostalgia when it comes, without overdoing it or leaving the period voice. The
nostalgia happens in the reader, never in the text: name the period's machines, programs, and places
as everyday facts of the present, and let an old-timer recognize them. Never reminisce ("remember
when...").

- **The detail must earn its place.** It should prove a point or give the reader something real, as
  in "Whether you use a Macintosh, an IBM PC, or a Sun workstation, you are welcome on the Internet,"
  where the machines show that protocols don't care what computer you use.
- **A few details at a time.** Three names make the point. Ten make a museum.
- **Name things the way the period did:** "a Sun workstation," "an IBM PC," "a Compact Macintosh."
- **Mark it EDGE** when a page has more than one or two such details, or when one is there mainly for
  the pleasure of it.

### Safety

Say once, calmly, on the Etiquette page, and in a sentence wherever a password comes up: these
protocols send everything as plain text, so anything sent can be read along the way. Use a password
you don't use anywhere else. Don't alarm the reader.

## Spelling and capitalization

- **the Internet**, **the Web**, **Web site**, **home page**, **e-mail**, **newsgroup**
- **the World Wide Web** in titles and on first mention on its own page ("the World Wide Web (the
  Web)"); **the Web** everywhere else
- **Macintosh** on first mention on a page, **Mac** after that. **Compact Macintosh** for the Plus,
  SE, and Classic family.
- Name protocols the way their rulebooks do: HTTP, FTP, Gopher, finger, whois, DICT, NNTP, IMAP,
  LDAP, SMB, NFS. In an address, use the lowercase scheme: `gopher://`.
- Name each protocol's RFC once, as its rulebook: "Gopher is described in RFC 1436, published in
  1993." Cite only RFCs published by 1997.

## Linked places

- **Every link is real and tested in Mill** before it is published, and checked again on a schedule
  (see *Testing*). A link that fails comes out until it works again.
- **Prefer places that last:** places run by communities, institutions, and dedicated individuals.
- **Describe each place in our voice.** The guide is not an enclave: the places it links to are real
  and may show their own time (dates, versions, words from after 1997). That is fine. Our own text
  stays true then and true now and never comments on what a linked site says about itself. Choose
  places for what they offer the reader; a first screen that sits comfortably in the period is a
  bonus, not a requirement.
- **Credit the people who run things.** Most servers worth visiting are kept by people who pay for
  them; say so, and teach the reader to be a good guest.
- **Search starts with FrogFind:** "a search engine that also rewrites the pages it finds so a
  smaller computer can read them."

## Pages and format

The guide is designed for Mill in a Macintosh Plus window (a 512 x 342 screen). Every page must also
read well in any other browser.

- **HTML 3.2**, validated against the HTML 3.2 DTD. No CSS, no JavaScript, no frames.
- **ISO 8859-1 only.** HTML 3.2 has no curly quotes and no `&mdash;`: use straight quotes and `--`
  or rewrite the sentence.
- **The first screen of every page is useful on its own.** About 18 lines of 12-point text fit in
  Mill's page area; measure against the real window before settling the numbers.
- **A fixed column.** Each page's content sits in one centered table cell, 460 pixels wide
  (`<TABLE WIDTH=460 ALIGN=CENTER BORDER=0 CELLPADDING=0 CELLSPACING=0><TR><TD>`), so lines stay
  a readable length on wide screens and fill Mill's window on a Plus. Check the width against
  Mill's page area.
- **One idea per screen.** Paragraphs of four lines or fewer on a Plus screen.
- **Under about 16 KB of HTML per page.**
- **Links to us:** the first mention of Mill on a page links to `http://mill.1in1e6.com/`, and the
  first mention of OneInAMillion Software links to `http://1in1e6.com/`. Later mentions are plain.
- **Images:**
  - Every image is published as a 1-bit GIF, converted before publishing; never rely on Mill to
    dither. Originals, in any format, are kept in `art/` (not served), and `tools/onebit.sh` makes
    the GIF from them.
  - Drawings, not photographs: bold lines and solid blacks, little fine hatching, which breaks up
    when shrunk.
  - Illustrations are single-panel cartoons in the style of a newspaper editorial cartoon, pen and
    ink. Let the drawing speak for itself: label only what can't be recognized without one.
    Lettering is added afterward in a bitmap typeface (Times Bold 14 reads cleanly; the 12's "8"
    looks like an "S"), so it stays crisp at 1 bit. Generated originals are asked for with no text.
  - A short italic caption under a cartoon carries its punchline.
  - Always give `WIDTH`, `HEIGHT`, and `ALT`.
  - Nothing wider than about 460 pixels.
- **Every address is shown in `<TT>`** as well as linked, so the reader can read it and type it.
  Reading an address is a skill the guide teaches.
- **Callouts:** Try this, Tip, Note, and Important are set apart the same way every time: a box
  with a solid border, the 32 x 32 icon and the bold label on one line at the top, and the text
  below running the full width of the box, inset slightly by the cell padding.

  ```html
  <P><TABLE WIDTH="100%" BORDER=1 CELLPADDING=6 CELLSPACING=0><TR><TD>
  <TABLE BORDER=0 CELLPADDING=0 CELLSPACING=0><TR><TD WIDTH=40><IMG SRC="images/tip.gif" WIDTH=32
  HEIGHT=32 ALT=""></TD><TD VALIGN=MIDDLE><B>Tip</B></TD></TR></TABLE>
  <P>...
  </TD></TR></TABLE>
  ```

  The icons are `try.gif` (Try this), `tip.gif` (Tip), `note.gif` (Note), and `important.gif`
  (Important, kept for safety: passwords and plain text). `book.gif` marks the Glossary and
  `where.gif` Where to Go. The icon's ALT is empty because the bold label right beside it says the
  same thing.
- **Navigation:** the same Contents | Previous | Next line, centered, at the bottom of every page
  only (screen space on a Plus is too scarce for a second copy at the top),
  following the reading order: Welcome, Contents, then the pages in the order of the outline. The
  list of pages is on the Contents page only; no other page repeats it. The current page's own
  entry in the line is plain text, not a link.
- **A blank line after the navigation** (`<BR>&nbsp;` at the end of the line), so that when the page
  is scrolled to the bottom, the links sit one line above Mill's status bar instead of under it. A
  `<BR>` alone at the end of a paragraph adds no height in many browsers; the `&nbsp;` gives the line
  something to hold.
- **File names:** short and lowercase (`gopher.html`), so they are easy to type.

## Marking judgment calls

Mark every humor call and every borderline true-then-true-now call in the source with
`<!-- EDGE: why -->`. Eric reviews each one before publishing. Running `grep -rn EDGE docs/` lists
them all.

Published pages contain no comments at all: Mill's View Source would show them. Each EDGE is settled
(kept or rewritten) and then removed before the page is pushed. `tools/check.sh` refuses any page
with a comment in it.

## Testing

- **Before every push:** `tools/check.sh`, which checks the DTD, ASCII, comments, size, and image
  attributes.
- **In Mill:** view every page in Mill at Macintosh Plus resolution before publishing.
- **Links:** check every link in the guide by speaking its protocol. curl handles http, ftp, gopher,
  dict, ldap, imap, and smb, and nc handles finger and time.
- **Record:** keep the date of each check in the repository, not on the pages.
